# CeylonPick — Feasibility Study & Risk Analysis
**Multi-vendor e-commerce + affiliate/referral platform, Sri Lanka**
Prepared: 5 September 2026 | Perspective: market research, financial analysis, e-commerce strategy
Budget constraint: minimal capital, marketing budget under LKR 50,000, zero tolerance for capital loss

---

## සාරාංශය (Executive Summary in brief)

CeylonPick "everything marketplace" එකක් විදිහට launch කළොත් අසාර්ථක වීමේ ඉඩකඩ ඉතා ඉහළයි (සාර්ථක වීමේ ඉඩ 5%ට අඩුයි). ඒත් project එක සම්පූර්ණයෙන් අත්හරින්න ඕන නෑ. Daraz සමඟ තරඟ කරන්නේ නැතුව, **Facebook/Instagram/TikTok හරහා විකුණන කුඩා දේශීය brands වලට "විශ්වාසය + creator distribution" ලබා දෙන curated, niche platform එකක්** විදිහට redesign කළොත් — සහ පහත දැඩි නීති අනුගමනය කළොත් — 18 මාසයකින් break-even වීමේ ඉඩකඩ 15–25% දක්වා වැඩි කරගන්න පුළුවන්. මුදල් අහිමි නොවීමට ප්‍රධාන නීතිය: **පළමු 90 දිනට LKR 150,000ට වඩා වියදම් නොකර, දින 90 kill-criteria එකක් තියාගෙන test කරන්න.**

## 0. The single most important finding

CeylonPick, as currently framed, has **three businesses stacked on top of each other**, each of which is individually hard in Sri Lanka:

1. A multi-vendor marketplace (a two-sided cold-start problem: no vendors without buyers, no buyers without vendors)
2. An affiliate network (a *third* side that also needs liquidity before it produces anything)
3. A COD-dependent logistics operation (cash-negative by design)

A LKR 50,000 marketing budget (~USD 165) cannot bootstrap even one of these sides. The 2024 Daraz/Alibaba restructuring and the 2026 regional layoffs (including roughly 30 staff cut in Sri Lanka) show that even a subsidised giant finds Sri Lankan unit economics difficult. Kapruka, founded in 2003 and listed in 2022, only returned to full-year profit in FY2026 (revenue LKR 1.92bn, PBT LKR 37.56m) after posting a LKR 114.6m net loss in FY2025 — and did so largely by pivoting to an asset-light marketplace and cross-border model.

**Conclusion up front:** the generalist marketplace idea should be killed. A narrowed version can be tested cheaply. The rest of this document explains what to keep, what to cut, and the rules that protect the capital.

## 1. Market Gap & Competitor Analysis

### 1.1 The landscape in numbers (2025–2026)

| Metric | Value | Source / note |
|---|---|---|
| Daraz share of SL e-commerce | ~46% | Nature/HSSC survey study, 2024 |
| All other local platforms combined | ~16% | Same study |
| daraz.lk monthly visits (Jun 2026) | ~1.02M, −4.4% MoM, +5.7% YoY | Semrush |
| aliexpress.com visits from SL (Jun 2026) | ~829K | Semrush — direct cross-border competitor for low-price goods |
| Shoppers preferring COD | 52% (up from 48%) | APIDM/Kelaniya Digital Outlook 2025 |
| Card usage | 35% (down from 39.5%) | Same |
| E-wallets | ≤1% | Same |
| Kapruka FY2026 revenue / PBT | LKR 1.92bn / LKR 37.56m | Daily FT, Jun 2026 |
| Kapruka FY2025 net result | −LKR 114.6m | Simply Wall St |

Two things stand out. First, the remaining ~38% of e-commerce is **not** on any platform — it is Facebook Marketplace, Instagram, WhatsApp and TikTok sellers taking bank transfers and COD. That "un-platformed" segment is the only realistic gap. Second, Daraz traffic is flat-to-declining while AliExpress traffic is large: Sri Lankans are already bypassing local platforms for cheap tech and fashion. Any niche that AliExpress serves well is closed to CeylonPick.

### 1.2 Why a vendor would NOT choose CeylonPick (be honest about this first)

- **Daraz** gives them buyer traffic on day one, Daraz-managed logistics (DEX), and a brand shoppers already trust with COD. A new platform offers none of this.
- **Facebook/Instagram/WhatsApp** are free, the seller owns the customer relationship, gets bank transfer or COD directly, and pays zero commission. This is the real incumbent for small sellers, not Daraz.
- **Kapruka Partner Central** now offers exactly what CeylonPick proposes — plug into an existing customer base, logistics and payments — with 20+ years of brand trust behind it.

A vendor will only list on CeylonPick if it does something these three cannot.

### 1.3 The pains CeylonPick can actually solve

Interviewing the Facebook-seller economy (the ~38%) surfaces three consistent complaints:

1. **Buyer trust deficit.** Small sellers lose sales because buyers fear scams (70–80% of mid-range beauty orders are COD precisely because buyers don't trust prepayment). A seller with 800 followers cannot manufacture trust alone.
2. **Distribution ceiling.** Organic reach on Meta has collapsed; boosting posts costs money the seller doesn't have. They need someone else to sell for them.
3. **Being invisible on Daraz.** On Daraz, a local handmade soap competes on the same page as a Chinese import at one-third the price. The platform commoditises them.

### 1.4 The USP that can survive

**"The trusted storefront for verified Sri Lankan-made brands, sold by creators."**

Concretely:

- **Curation, not openness.** Maximum 30–50 vendors at launch, each physically verified (NIC/BR check, product sample inspected, one video call). The badge "Verified Sri Lankan Maker" is the product. Daraz cannot copy this without breaking its own model.
- **Creator-first distribution.** Every affiliate gets a personal storefront page (ceylonpick.lk/@name) with their curated picks. Sri Lanka does **not** have TikTok Shop, Instagram Checkout, or LTK. Creators who want to monetise product recommendations currently have no clean local tool. That vacuum is the strongest argument for CeylonPick's existence.
- **Trust layer for buyers.** Escrow-style handling: buyer pays CeylonPick (or COD is collected by CeylonPick's courier account), vendor is paid after delivery confirmation. Buyers are protected from the individual seller; sellers are protected from fake orders by CeylonPick's phone verification.

Do not position as "cheaper than Daraz", "more products than Daraz", or "Sri Lankan Daraz". You will lose on all three.

## 2. Affiliate & Creator Ecosystem in Sri Lanka

### 2.1 Current behaviour

- **Volume exists, monetisation does not.** Sri Lanka has thousands of TikTok creators in the 10K–500K follower range (StarNgage tracks 1,000+ ranked accounts). Most earn via occasional flat-fee brand posts (LKR 5,000–50,000 per post for micro/mid-tier), giveaways, or nothing at all.
- **Affiliate literacy is low but rising.** Local "make money online" content increasingly covers the Daraz Affiliate Program (which now operates in Sri Lanka, with rate tables only visible inside the dashboard) and Amazon Associates. Creators understand the concept; they distrust the payout.
- **Flat fee is preferred over performance.** A creator who can get LKR 15,000 guaranteed for one post will not take a pure-commission deal from an unknown platform. They have been burned by "we'll pay you when it sells" promises from small brands.
- **Payout friction.** Bank transfers are fine; anything under LKR 5,000 per month is not worth their attention.

### 2.2 The 30% problem — the maths does not work as stated

If CeylonPick charges vendors a 10–12% platform commission and gives affiliates 30% *of that commission*, the creator earns roughly 3–3.6% of the product price.

| Product price | Platform commission (12%) | Affiliate share (30% of commission) | Sales needed for LKR 30,000/month |
|---|---|---|---|
| LKR 1,500 | LKR 180 | LKR 54 | 556 delivered sales |
| LKR 3,000 | LKR 360 | LKR 108 | 278 delivered sales |
| LKR 6,000 | LKR 720 | LKR 216 | 139 delivered sales |

No micro-influencer in Sri Lanka will drive 278 delivered COD orders per month for an unknown platform for LKR 30,000. They will post once, see LKR 400 in their dashboard, and leave. **The 30%-of-commission structure will kill the affiliate side within 60 days.**

### 2.3 What will work

- **Vendor-funded affiliate commission of 10–20% of product price**, not a share of the platform's cut. The vendor sets the rate (minimum 10%); CeylonPick takes a flat 5–7% on top. Vendors in artisan/personalised/beauty categories have 50–70% gross margins and can afford this — they are already paying more than that in boosted posts.
- **Tiered guarantees for the first 20 "founding creators":** a small fixed retainer in product (not cash — vendors donate samples), plus commission. This is your LKR 50,000: product seeding, not ads.
- **Pay on delivered + 7 days, twice a month, minimum LKR 2,000.** Publish the schedule and hit it every single time. One late payment is fatal to reputation in a small creator community where everyone talks.
- **Give creators the storefront and the data.** Real-time clicks, orders, RTO — visible in a simple dashboard. Transparency is the trust substitute for a brand they've never heard of.
- **Recruit from the un-monetised middle**: 5K–50K follower creators in Sinhala/Tamil-language lifestyle, home, food, beauty and student niches. Ignore anyone above 200K — they want flat fees you cannot pay.

### 2.4 Will they trust a local platform?

Not initially. Trust is earned by: (a) named, visible founders; (b) a public payout log; (c) the first 5 creators publicly confirming they were paid. Budget zero for "trust marketing" — budget time for hand-holding 20 creators on WhatsApp.

## 3. Financial & Operational Bottlenecks

### 3.1 The payment landscape

- **PayHere Lite** (the default gateway for SL SMEs): free setup, roughly 3.3% per card transaction, transaction cap around LKR 50,000 and ~LKR 200,000/month; Plus tier is ~LKR 3,990/month at 2.99% with higher limits. HelaPay local rail at ~1.99%. Requires a registered business — register as a sole proprietor at the Divisional Secretariat before anything else.
- **Cards ~35%, e-wallets ≤1%, BNPL ~3%, COD ~52%.** Assume 55–65% of your orders will be COD for at least the first year regardless of what you do, and higher in beauty and anything under LKR 3,000.
- Courier COD pricing: base LKR 200–400 per parcel within Colombo plus a COD collection surcharge of LKR 50–150. Outstation adds more. Failed deliveries return to origin (RTO), and remittance to sellers is on a 1–3 day cycle — with remittance delays being the most-reported seller complaint.

### 3.2 COD cash flow — the model that actually bleeds

Assume 100 orders, average basket LKR 2,500, 60% COD, RTO rate on COD of 25% (a realistic Sri Lankan figure for a new, unknown store; established players see 10–20%).

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Orders placed | 100 |
| Prepaid orders (40) — RTO ~3% | 39 delivered |
| COD orders (60) — RTO 25% | 45 delivered, 15 returned |
| Total delivered | 84 |
| Courier cost, all 100 shipments (~LKR 350 avg) | LKR 35,000 |
| Return shipping on 15 RTO (~LKR 250) | LKR 3,750 |
| Gross platform commission at 6% × 84 × LKR 2,500 | LKR 12,600 |
| Affiliate commission (vendor-funded, so zero to platform) | 0 |

If CeylonPick absorbs shipping (as buyers expect from Daraz), the platform loses ~LKR 26,000 on 100 orders before paying for hosting, PayHere fees, or a single hour of the founder's time. **Every rule below exists to change this table.**

### 3.3 How returns break the affiliate structure

If affiliates are paid on *order placed*, a creator (or a friend) can place 50 COD orders with fake numbers, collect commission, and let the parcels RTO. This is the standard fraud in every COD market (Pakistan, Bangladesh, Egypt) and it will happen here within the first month.

**Non-negotiable rules:**

1. Commission accrues only on **delivered + 7-day non-return** status, confirmed by courier API/webhook, not by the vendor.
2. Payout holds for 14 days after delivery; two payout cycles per month.
3. Any affiliate with RTO > 30% on their own orders is auto-paused; > 40% is removed and unpaid commission forfeited (state this in the T&Cs from day one).
4. COD orders require OTP phone verification before dispatch; orders that don't confirm within 24 hours are cancelled — the affiliate sees "not confirmed", not "sale".
5. COD cap of LKR 5,000 per order for the first six months; above that, prepay only or 30% advance via PayHere.

### 3.4 Other bottlenecks people forget

- **Vendor payout timing.** You are holding vendors' money. Pay them on a published 7-day post-delivery cycle. Late vendor payments destroy the supply side faster than anything.
- **Returns policy conflict.** Daraz trains buyers to expect 7–14 day free returns. Small makers cannot absorb that. Set a clear "damaged/wrong item only" policy and state it on every product page.
- **Tax.** Keep records from day one; VAT registration threshold and SSCL apply once revenue grows. Not a launch problem, but a 12-month problem.
- **Tech cost trap.** A custom React/Laravel multi-vendor app with an affiliate engine costs LKR 800,000–2,500,000 to build properly. The budget does not allow this. Use WooCommerce + a multi-vendor plugin (Dokan/WCFM) + an affiliate plugin + the PayHere WooCommerce plugin on shared hosting (LKR 15,000–30,000/year). Or launch as a single curated store with vendors on consignment and add "multi-vendor" software only after 500 delivered orders.

## 4. Micro-Niche Go-To-Market Strategy (Under LKR 50,000)

### 4.1 Niches to REJECT (and why)

- **Tech accessories / gadgets:** AliExpress pulls ~829K SL visits a month and Daraz dominates the rest; margins are 10–20%, fakes are rampant, warranty disputes are constant. You cannot win here.
- **Fashion apparel:** highest return rate of any category (size/colour), and the Facebook seller economy is already efficient at it.
- **Electronics/appliances:** Singer, Abans, Softlogic and Daraz. Capital intensive, low margin.
- **Groceries/FMCG:** logistics-heavy, low basket, Keells/Cargills/Daraz Mart.

### 4.2 The three niches to launch with

**Niche 1 — Sri Lankan artisan consumables & gifting hampers**
Ceylon tea (single-estate, flavoured), spices, kithul treacle and jaggery, bee honey, natural soaps, herbal balms, small-batch snacks — bundled into hampers for birthdays, Avurudu, Vesak, Christmas, corporate gifting.
- *Why:* light, non-fragile, low RTO, repeat purchase, high gift-ability (gifts are frequently prepaid because the buyer isn't the recipient), 50–70% vendor margin supports 15% affiliate commission. Strong "Sri Lankan-made" story that Daraz cannot tell.
- *Risk:* food-grade labelling and shelf life; require vendors to have basic packaging compliance.
- *Creator fit:* food, home, lifestyle and "Sri Lankan pride" content; corporate HR/admin pages for hampers.

**Niche 2 — Personalised & made-to-order gifts (prepaid only)**
Engraved wood/acrylic items, printed mugs, custom illustrations, name-embroidered items, couple/baby/graduation gifts.
- *Why:* personalisation *justifies* prepayment — nobody expects COD on an item with their name on it. This is the one category where you can push COD below 20% from day one. Margins 60%+. Occasion-driven, so creators have a natural content hook (anniversary, exam results, Valentine's).
- *Risk:* production lead time and quality disputes; enforce a 5-working-day SLA and photo proof before dispatch.
- *Creator fit:* couple content, student/university creators, wedding and baby pages.

**Niche 3 — Local natural beauty & wellness micro-brands**
Ayurveda-based hair oils, herbal face packs, natural deodorants, small-batch skincare from registered local brands.
- *Why:* beauty is where Sri Lankan creators already are, and where affiliate content converts best globally. Local brands are drowning on Daraz next to Korean and Chinese imports; a "verified local, ingredients-transparent" storefront is a genuine differentiator.
- *Risk:* this is the highest-COD category (70–80% of mid-range beauty is COD), so apply the strict COD rules above and cap COD at LKR 3,000 here. Require vendors to hold cosmetics registration where applicable.
- *Creator fit:* the largest pool of micro-influencers in the country.

### 4.3 How to spend LKR 50,000 (do not spend it on Meta ads)

| Allocation | LKR | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Product seeding to 20 founding creators (co-funded by vendors) | 20,000 | Their first video is your entire launch campaign |
| Photography/props for 30 products (one weekend, one DSLR) | 8,000 | Bad product photos are the #1 conversion killer |
| Domain + hosting + SSL (1 year) | 12,000 | WooCommerce stack, not a custom app |
| Printed thank-you cards + branded tape | 5,000 | Repeat orders and unboxing content |
| Reserve for RTO losses in month 1 | 5,000 | Never spend the reserve |
| **Total** | **50,000** | |

Business registration, PayHere onboarding, and the founder's time are outside this figure but are mandatory.

### 4.4 Geographic focus

Colombo, Gampaha, Kalutara only for the first 90 days. Same-day/next-day delivery at LKR 200–350 is achievable; RTO is lower; you can personally handle disputes. Island-wide expansion happens only after 300 delivered orders.

## 5. Verdict & Success Rate

### 5.1 Realistic probabilities (analytical estimate)

There is no published dataset tracking Sri Lankan e-commerce startup survival, so any percentage is a triangulated expert estimate, not a measured statistic. The triangulation uses: global e-commerce startup failure data (roughly 80–90% of new online stores do not reach profitability within two years), the local track record (wow.lk raised ~USD 1.59m with Dialog backing and was still absorbed; Kaymu became Daraz; Takas fought payment infrastructure for years; Kapruka needed two decades and a public listing to turn a modest profit), and the specific constraints of this project.

| Scenario | Probability of surviving 12 months | Probability of break-even within 18 months |
|---|---|---|
| **As currently designed** — open multi-vendor "everything" marketplace, 30%-of-commission affiliate model, LKR 50,000 marketing | 8–12% | **2–5%** |
| **Redesigned** — curated 3-niche storefront, vendor-funded 10–20% affiliate commission, creator storefronts, strict COD/RTO rules, WooCommerce stack, Western Province only, 90-day kill criteria | 40–50% | **15–25%** |

Even the redesigned version is a minority outcome. What changes is the *cost of being wrong*: in the redesigned version, failure is discovered by day 90 for under LKR 150,000 total outlay, instead of by month 8 after a LKR 1.5m app build.

### 5.2 Top 3 reasons platforms like this fail in the first 6 months

**1. The empty-marketplace death spiral.**
The founder recruits 40 vendors with promises of traffic; traffic doesn't come; vendors stop updating stock; the first buyers see "out of stock" and never return; affiliates see zero conversions and leave. All three sides quit within 90 days.
*How CeylonPick avoids it:* launch with 15–25 vendors you have personally met and 20 creators who have physically received product. Solve demand for one niche before adding the next. Do not open vendor self-registration until month 6.

**2. COD/RTO cash bleed and affiliate fraud.**
Free shipping to compete with Daraz + 25–30% RTO + paying affiliates on order placement = negative gross margin on every order. The business runs out of cash while showing "growth" in orders.
*How CeylonPick avoids it:* the five COD rules in Section 3.3, buyer-paid shipping (or vendor-subsidised, never platform-subsidised), and a weekly RTO dashboard as the founder's most-watched number.

**3. Building the product before proving the demand.**
Founders spend 4–6 months and most of the capital on a custom app with a beautiful affiliate dashboard, launch to silence, and have no money left for the actual work of recruiting vendors and creators.
*How CeylonPick avoids it:* WooCommerce, a plugin stack, and a Google Sheet for affiliate payouts in month one. The "platform" is the founder's WhatsApp for the first 90 days. Build software only for problems you have already had ten times.

### 5.3 Strict rules to follow (the capital-protection covenant)

1. **Total cash at risk in the first 90 days ≤ LKR 150,000** (including the LKR 50,000 marketing). No exceptions, no "just one more month".
2. **Day-90 kill criteria — stop or pivot if any of these is true:** fewer than 150 delivered orders; RTO above 25%; fewer than 8 creators who have earned a payout; repeat-purchase rate below 10%.
3. **No custom software until 500 delivered orders.**
4. **No platform-paid shipping, ever, in year one.**
5. **Affiliates are paid only on delivered + 7 days; vendors on delivered + 7 days; both on a published calendar you never miss.**
6. **COD cap LKR 5,000 (LKR 3,000 in beauty); OTP confirmation before dispatch; personalised items prepaid only.**
7. **Three niches, Western Province, max 50 SKUs per niche at launch.** Expansion requires 300 delivered orders in the current scope.
8. **Verify every vendor in person or on video.** One fake-product scandal ends the "trusted" positioning permanently.
9. **Track four numbers weekly:** delivered orders, RTO %, contribution margin per order after courier and gateway fees, and creator payout total. If contribution margin per delivered order is not positive by week 8, the model is wrong.
10. **The founder personally handles every complaint for six months.** Trust is the product; there is no budget to buy it.

### 5.4 Final word

The affiliate-driven marketplace thesis is sound in principle — Sri Lanka has creators with no monetisation tool and small makers with no distribution. The problem is not the idea; it is the shape and the budget. Shrink it until LKR 150,000 can test it fully, run the 90-day test with the kill criteria written down before you start, and treat survival past day 90 as permission to raise the next LKR 500,000 — not as proof of success.
